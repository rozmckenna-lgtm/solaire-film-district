<div><section class="open-letter-page" id="r6yd2t8lvpd0ttj" image-layout="blank"><grid id="SIEOamrzYY" spacing="none"><grid-item id="jYGrXNuurU"><h1>Does a Story Have to Be Fast to Be Compelling?</h1><p><i>Why intelligent dialogue, suspense and emotional depth deserve room to develop</i></p><grid id="iT720DoqPu" vertical-sizing="hug" spacing="none"><grid-item padding="12pt 16pt" background-color="surface" id="S_LE1Ob5GE"><p><b>Quick Answer</b></p><p>No. A scene holds attention when something important is at stake, even if the characters remain seated and speak quietly. A discovery, a change of allegiance or a question left unanswered can be as gripping as a chase. The writer's task is to give each development the time it needs.</p></grid-item></grid><h2>Why does quiet drama hold our attention?</h2><p>In <i>Elementary</i>, much of the pleasure lies in watching Holmes and Watson notice, question and reason. A small inconsistency can overturn an entire theory. The mystery gives viewers something to solve alongside the characters. There may be a body, a pursuit or a confrontation, but those moments do not have to dominate the episode.</p><p>Quiet drama also gives dialogue room for precision and humour. People who listen carefully, speak eloquently and disagree without shouting can be fascinating company. Calm delivery does not diminish the consequences of what is being said.</p><h2>What actually makes a scene dramatic?</h2><p>A useful test comes from David Mamet's memo to the writers of <i>The Unit</i>: identify what a character wants, what happens if they do not get it, and why the matter must be dealt with now.</p><p>Imagine a detective asking a witness about a missing hour. The witness has an explanation. The detective knows one detail that contradicts it but does not immediately reveal what she knows. Both characters have an objective, and every answer changes their position. The tension comes from the contest between them, not the volume of their voices.</p></grid-item></grid></section><section class="open-letter-page" id="kvrorpbyfpq3rhr" image-layout="blank"><grid id="aVHkDsCm-H" spacing="none"><grid-item id="V92Z_PVTUV"><h1>When should a scene be allowed to breathe?</h1><hr width="xs" thickness="sm" color="accent"><p>Script consultant Justine Hart recently described the absence of what she calls 'settle-in scenes' in some spec scripts. Her observation is useful when applied to relationships. A friendship cannot convincingly disintegrate merely because the plot requires it. We may need to see the small concessions, evasions and discoveries that bring it to breaking point.</p><p>Allowing time for that progression is different from retaining repetitive exchanges. Each part of the scene should alter our understanding or the characters' choices.</p><h2>Can silence do the work of dialogue?</h2><p>Yes, when viewers know enough to interpret it. A woman discovers that her partner has lied, then continues an ordinary dinner conversation without mentioning the discovery. We watch what she notices, what she chooses not to say and how he responds. Silence becomes an action because withholding the truth is a decision.</p><p>Without context, however, silence may simply be empty screen time. The writer must establish what the audience is waiting to learn.</p><h2>How do you decide what to cut?</h2><p>Robert McKee's approach to scene design offers a practical question: does the scene turn? A character might begin confident and leave doubtful, enter as an ally and depart as an opponent, or discover information that makes an earlier choice impossible.</p><p>If nothing changes, consider whether the scene belongs in the screenplay. If the change occurs halfway through, consider whether the remaining dialogue adds a necessary consequence. Conversely, if cutting a conversation removes the moment when trust breaks or a decision becomes inevitable, the cut has gone too far.</p><p>Pacing is also a matter of contrast. A restrained exchange may be particularly effective after a crisis; a sudden interruption may have greater force when the audience has settled into a quieter rhythm.</p></grid-item></grid></section><section class="open-letter-page" id="ohgc60opxk3yb9j" image-layout="blank"><grid id="rT1Scg3DyN" spacing="none"><grid-item id="Iaj_nwr-S-"><h1>What can screenwriters learn from their own viewing habits?</h1><hr width="xs" thickness="sm" color="accent"><p>Notice what makes you continue watching. Is it fear for a character, the pleasure of solving a puzzle, the humour of an exchange, or the anticipation of a difficult choice? Different genres and audiences favour different combinations.</p><p>Writers are often told to make a script faster. A more precise instruction is to make its dramatic purpose clearer. Some scenes should end almost as soon as they begin. Others deserve the time it takes for a person to realise that everything has changed.</p><spacer mode="fill"></spacer><h3 class="pull-quote" role="subheading" font-size="28pt"><b>The useful question is not 'Can this scene be shorter?' but 'At what point has this scene done its work?'</b></h3><spacer mode="fill"></spacer><h3>Further reading</h3><p>David Mamet, <i>Memo to the Writers of The Unit</i>; Robert McKee, <i>Story</i> and his writing on scene turns; John August and Craig Mazin, <i>Scriptnotes</i>, discussions of scene transitions and rhythm. Justine Hart's October 2026 LinkedIn observation on 'settle-in scenes' provided the starting point for this discussion.</p><hr thickness="sm" color="neutral-border"><p><i>An article for the Solaire PitchUp Creator Centre.</i></p></grid-item></grid></section></div>
